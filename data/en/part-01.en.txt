Preface.

"With what shall I come before the Lord, and bow myself before God on high" [Mic 6:6], who guided me from my mother's womb [Ps 22:11], kept me at all times in His kindness, called me to His service these twenty-seven years, and strengthened me to meditate on His Torah and delight in it? This is my portion of all my labor [Eccl 2:10]: that a pleasant heritage fell to my lot [Ps 16:6], and my line fell in the field of the offerings of the Torah; and even on the day of my distress I knew that my Redeemer lives [Job 19:25], and that His word for ever will light my darkness [Ps 18:29]. For some years now I have consecrated the dew of my youth, from the womb of the dawn [Ps 110:3], to inquiring, for the glory of the awesome and terrible One, into the Mishnah that our forebears bequeathed to us. It is our eternal inheritance from our fathers, the foundation stone and cornerstone of our faith; on it hang the offspring and the issue [Isa 22:24]; from it shines a precious light, and it is the source of the streams of salvation. But one thing I laid to heart, and trembling seized me; shuddering came upon me, and horror covered me [Ps 55:6]. Is not the Torah, given to us as an inheritance, a witness among us and in its very essence for ever, for it came from the mouth of the Lord of hosts and is called by the name of the eternal God? From of old many have wondered at the sight and searched for the meaning of the vision: why "the bush is not consumed" [Exod 3:2], though it burns time after time with a devouring fire. This is Israel, whom the flame of fire ⚠1 on every side, while his enemies and foes gnashed their teeth at him [Lam 2:16] from of old. But if they were wise they would understand, and bring gifts to the One to be feared [Ps 76:12]: in the burning bush is the fire of God; in it is the shout of a King [Num 23:21] and the spirit of God from on high; it clothed him with strength and might to stand in the day of trouble and wrath. Some trust in spear and bow and shield, and some in the thick bosses of the shield of faith [Job 15:26]; some in tumult and the shout of battle, and some in trust in the Lord, in quietness and stillness. They are finished and consumed in the darkness of night, while Israel takes courage and does valiantly [Num 24:18]. Happy is he! The Lord has spread over him the banner of His peace; the living God has set on him the mark [Ezek 9:4], a sign of life, to raise him up. And even if around him yesterday there were ruin and desolation [Job 30:3], distress and gloom and fearful darkness, is there not within him an eternal light, which shines hidden and concealed? Outside, the sound of tumult and outcry; within, quiet and joy. Outside, the cruel voice cries bitterly [Zeph 1:14]; and in the tents of Jacob is the voice of those who study the Torah, quiet and glad. By the radiance of the Torah they walked at liberty [Ps 119:45]; they soared high above trouble and sorrow; they meditated in it, and there was light in their souls; their spirit and their thought were sated with the dew of the lights of light. Therefore this is the glory that our fathers bequeathed to us, for an everlasting splendor to us and our children: the child of wisdom and understanding, the fruit of the depth of thought and discernment, a name better than sons [Isa 56:5], an inheritance more precious than pearls [Prov 3:15]; as if to say: happy is the man whose strength is in it [Ps 84:6], and who makes Torah and the fear of God his stronghold. — At this I awoke, and my spirit was troubled [Gen 41:8]; I rejoiced at the sight, and yet my complaint was bitter [Job 23:2]. How precious is our Torah, and how pleasant our lot! Why then should the beginning of all wisdom be lacking, and the precious cornerstone of all knowledge be hidden from us? The Mishnah is the foundation of the Torah; and one thing, and that a great one, is missing in it: the connection and joining between its parts, the coherence and order of its details. The house too is shut, that none may enter [Isa 24:10], for the door and the entrance cannot be found. And it came to pass, as the days grew long and the Lord lit up my eyes from on high, that I saw in some tractate a paved road going up straight, step by step. Then my spirit came back to me, my soul exulted within me, and I said: the light is bright in the skies [Job 37:21], only my eyes are too dim to see the path in the depths [Isa 44:18]; I must search, inquire and meditate; perhaps the Lord will show me wonders out of His Torah [Ps 119:18], and I may be worthy to come into the sanctuary, where the precious place of the Mishnah is. And the Lord [helps] me; therefore I was not confounded [Isa 50:7]. I found a little here, a little there [Isa 28:10], and gathered them one by one. And I answered and said:

The early sages winnowed and sifted and ground and brought out fine flour, for their heart was as wide as the door of the hall [Eruvin 53a], and by the spirit of their understanding they knew all the foundations of the Mishnah. Their way of study, too, was in proper order: to go up from the easy to the difficult, to draw out general rules and to set up signposts [Jer 31:21], and the like. By this their way of study is known: all the gates of the Torah were open before them, not only the Mishnah and the two Talmuds, but also the Tosefta and the Mekhilta, the Sifra and the Sifre, and the midrashim too. If a man gave all the wealth of his house [Song 8:7] he would not attain this glory. But there is only one way, the king's highway: to explore with wisdom by beginning with the Mishnah and knowing its hidden things; to consider in one's soul how its words are tied to one another, how the sayings of the Tosefta and so on cling to it, and how the masters of the Talmud reasoned about it with wisdom. The beginning of all wisdom is to come in by the gate of wisdom; and whoever leaps into the chambers of wisdom through the window, and does not come by way of the gate, his blood is on his own head [Josh 2:19], if he labors in vain and brings forth for terror [Isa 65:23]. The light of our eyes, the great eagle, our master Moses son of Maimon, when he saw that some men of his generation erred in this matter, made the effort and set before the children of Israel his honored introduction to the Mishnah, and in it showed how the gate to the Oral Torah is found. Would that they had walked by his light! Then knowledge would have increased [Dan 12:4], and the Torah would not be hidden, in many of its corners, even from those who study it day by day; most of the orders of Zera'im, Kodashim and Toharot would not be to them as a sealed book [Isa 29:11]; they would not have failed to set their eyes on the Tosefta and its companions, or never knocked at the doors of the Jerusalem Talmud. The introduction of Maimonides, great as it is in its matter, did not suffice to satisfy the longing soul [Ps 107:9] that thirsts for knowledge, especially in the present state of learning in our time. His rules on the subject of "a law given to Moses at Sinai" have already been attacked by many; in the order of the tannaim that he gathered in ten chapters one may find fault; and several foundations he did not consider, because in his generation no one tried to inquire into them. He also held back from inquiring into the redactors of the Mishnah, how it was arranged and who fixed the bounds of its readings; and many important inquiries are missing in his introduction.

And I, the least, although I knew the shortness of my strength, said: it is time to act for the Lord [Ps 119:126]. It also fell to my lot from on high to meditate on this honored study, for I was called to spread the word of the Lord among choice young men in the great house of study (the Seminar) that the late noble, our teacher R. Jonah Fraenckel Teomim, left behind him as a blessing by his will. I did not turn back, for I said: this is from the Lord [Ps 118:23], to renew the glory of the Torah and to raise up its splendor, which had almost been gathered away, when many called her an outcast whom no one seeks [Jer 30:17]. Then I left the beloved of my soul [Jer 12:7] and forsook the heritage I loved: the dear and honored holy community of Dresden, where I stood in the holy service eighteen years; my soul is bound up with the soul of its people, and they are engraved on the tablet of my heart. And I let myself be sought by those who asked me [Isa 65:1] to stand at the head of this great school. In this I trusted, that the purpose of the Lord would prosper in my hand [Isa 53:10], for He brought before me young men longing to hear the word of the Lord and to watch at the doors of His Torah [Prov 8:34]. Besides the lessons in the Torah that I set for them, I thought it right to lay before them an introduction to the Mishnah, to show them the depth of the Mishnah and the foundation of study, and to stir their souls to come into the gates of the nobles [Isa 13:2].

When I came to inquire into the Mishnah, I saw that the inquiry must be widened. I toiled and labored, as far as my strength went, in the old baraitot in which the source of the Mishnah is sometimes found: the Tosefta, the Mekhilta, the Sifra and the Sifre. Then I said: I will come with the roll of a book [Ps 40:8], so that these things may be a memorial for my students; and perhaps the Lord will be gracious to me and they will be of use to others too. — I have divided the book into three parts:

The first part contains the introduction to the Mishnah.

The second part will teach the order of the Mishnah in each tractate separately, the earlier and the later mishnayot, and the readings of the Mishnah.

The third part will deal with the Tosefta, the Mekhilta, the Sifra and the Sifre, and various other inquiries will come in it.

In this first part I have sometimes been brief, because I chose to bring the matters in their place in parts 2 and 3. — This part has also passed the limit that I set for it; therefore I was brief in the essays that appeared in my own journal, and besides, why should I repeat what has already been said and expounded elsewhere? — On some of the history of the Mishnah I wrote in the essay that I wrote when the gates of the Seminar were opened, and called it Über palästinische und alexandrinische Schriftforschung; I refer to it when I say "see the Programm".

I thought I should also mention this: the book was printed in a city far from where I live, and the workman at the press does not know the nature of Hebrew writing and language. As a result errors crept in, in letters that look alike, he and ḥet and so on, and sometimes a letter was carried over from one word to the next, and the like. The understanding reader will correct such errors himself; the errors in proper names and references we have listed at the end of the book.

With this I make an end of words [Job 18:2] and set my prayer before the Lord [Ps 5:4]: may He light my eyes with the light of His Torah, and guide me in the way of truth, the God of truth.

Breslau, in the month of Nisan 5619 [April 1859]. The author.
